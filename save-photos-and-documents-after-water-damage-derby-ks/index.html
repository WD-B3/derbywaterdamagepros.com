Save Photos and Documents After Water Damage in Derby, KS

Wet photographs and papers can often be saved if you act within a day or two. Here are the steps, in order.

By the Derby Water Damage Pros team · Published November 9, 2026

Wet photographs, certificates and records are often more precious than the furniture around them, and many can be saved if you move quickly. Mold and sticking begin to set in within a day or two, so the first hours matter.

This guide explains how to handle paper, photos and books, what you can do at home and when to ask for help. It also covers how to build your own photo record of the loss.

Handle with care

Wet paper is fragile. Touch it as little as possible and support it from below. Do not peel apart sheets that are stuck together. Do not use a hair dryer or direct heat, which can warp prints and bake in stains.

Work in a clean, dry area with good airflow. Wear gloves if the water was dirty. If the water was sewage or floodwater, items may be contaminated and a professional should advise you. See sewage backup cleanup for why caution is needed.

Air drying photographs

For photos that are still separate, lay them face up on clean paper towels or blotting paper. Change the towels when they get damp. Keep them out of direct sun. Photos stuck to glass can sometimes be soaked and separated gently in clean water, but only if the image side is not already sticking.

Do not wipe the image surface. If a print is stuck to another, stop and ask a conservator or a photo restoration service for advice.

Documents and books

Interleave wet book pages with paper towels, standing the book on its top edge to drain. Spread loose documents on a clean surface with space between them. If you cannot deal with everything within a day or two, place sheets between wax paper and freeze them, because freezing stops mold and gives you time.

Important originals, such as deeds, birth certificates and passports, can often be replaced from the issuing office. Keep a list of what was damaged, since replacement is usually easier with a record.

What to photograph for your claim

Take wide photos of each wet room, then close ups of damaged items. Photograph the source of the water if you can see it. Take a short video that walks through the space and narrates what you see. Photograph serial numbers, labels and any model plates.

Keep the originals of these images in the cloud or on a second device. Your insurer may ask to see them, so keep copies in two places.

Protect what you cannot save today

The water category in ANSI/IICRC S500 helps here: papers that met clean water have far better odds than papers that met sewage or floodwater.

If you must leave papers in a wet room, lift them onto a high shelf or into plastic bags. Moving them away from the water slows the damage. Tell the restoration crew which items matter to you, and they can work around them.

Prevention for next time

Store key papers and photos in waterproof containers above floor level. Scan the most important ones and keep copies in the cloud. A fire resistant, water resistant box is a cheap safeguard. These steps matter most in basements and garages, where many Derby families keep boxes of family history.

Framed art, albums and scrapbooks

Take framed pieces out of their frames if the glass is wet, because glass traps moisture against the print. Lay the artwork flat on a clean surface. Album pages with plastic sleeves can trap water, so open each sleeve gently and let air reach the prints.

Scrapbooks use glue, stickers and different papers, and they dry unevenly. Interleave pages with absorbent paper, and replace it often. If a scrapbook is irreplaceable, freezing it and asking a conservator for advice is a sensible choice.

Digital copies are the best insurance

Once your papers are safe, make a habit of scanning key documents. A phone camera is good enough for most records. Store the scans in two places, such as a cloud account and an external drive kept somewhere else. Name files clearly so you can find them in a hurry.

Photos on a phone or a computer can be harmed by water too, so back them up regularly. The same applies to tax records, policies and receipts. When the next emergency arrives, a scan is easier to send to an insurer than a damp original.

Derby sources for replacement and advice

Derby has a public library and the Derby Historical Museum, which is housed in a historic school building. Local institutions like these can be a good place to ask about preserving old photographs and records. For replacement of vital records, contact the issuing county or state office. If a loss is large, ask your insurer whether scanning or restoration of documents is covered, since policies differ.

When to call a professional

A conservator or document restoration service can freeze dry paper, treat mold and stabilize delicate items. They are worth the cost for irreplaceable pieces. For the building itself, call a restoration crew. The line is open 24/7, and a water extraction crew can pull out standing water while you look after the paper.

Check your policy

Some policies limit coverage for papers, photographs and keepsakes, and others require a rider for high value items. Ask your insurer before you decide what to discard. Coverage for keepsakes differs from coverage for the building.

Common questions

How long do I have to save wet photographs?

Act within a day or two if you can. Mold and sticking increase after that, so freeze papers if you cannot dry them right away.

Can I use a hair dryer on wet photos?

No. Heat can warp prints and set stains. Air dry them on clean absorbent paper instead.

Should I freeze wet documents?

Freezing can stop mold and buy time for papers you cannot dry at once. Place sheets between wax paper before freezing.

What should I photograph for my insurer?

Take wide and close up photos of every wet room and item, plus labels and serial numbers, and a short walk through video.
