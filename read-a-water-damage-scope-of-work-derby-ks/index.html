How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Derby, KS

A scope of work is the contract in plain language. Learn what each section means and what to ask before you sign.

By the Derby Water Damage Pros team · Published November 9, 2026

A scope of work is the written list of what a contractor will do, where and with what materials. It is the document that settles disagreements later, so reading it carefully before you sign is worth the time. Water damage scopes use shorthand that can look confusing, but the pieces repeat from job to job. This guide explains the typical sections and the questions that clear up most confusion.

Header and loss details

The top of a scope names the property, the date of loss, the source of water and the category and class of the loss. Check that the address and date are right. Check that the source matches what happened, since the source decides the category. A clean supply line and a sewer backup lead to very different plans.

Rooms and measurements

Scopes list each affected room with measurements in square feet or linear feet. Compare these with your own sense of the damage. If a room is missing, tell the contractor. If a measurement looks too large or small, ask how it was taken.

Mitigation tasks

Mitigation lines cover water extraction, removal of wet padding or flooring, cutting drywall at a height and cleaning. Look for the height of any drywall cut, such as two feet or four feet, and whether insulation will be removed. Our extraction page explains the first stage.

Drying equipment and monitoring

This section lists air movers, dehumidifiers and any specialty tools, with expected days. It should state how often readings are taken. Ask for the drying goal and for copies of the readings. The structural drying page explains what the numbers mean.

Materials to be replaced

A scope often separates what will be removed from what will be replaced. If you read only removal lines, you may be missing the rebuild. Ask whether replacement is in this scope or a later one, and who does it. See carpet restoration for an example of a remove or save decision.

Units and abbreviations

Common abbreviations include SF for square feet, LF for linear feet, EA for each and R&R for remove and replace. Ask the contractor to spell out anything you do not recognize. If a line is a lump sum, ask what it includes.

Exclusions and allowances

Exclusions list what is not included. Allowances are budget amounts for items to be chosen later, such as flooring. If the scope has an allowance, ask how an overage is handled and who approves changes.

A worked example

Suppose a scope lists extraction in a 12 by 14 room, removal of two feet of drywall on three walls, removal of wet padding, and four air movers and one dehumidifier for three days. That tells you the area, the cut height, the material removed and the equipment. It does not tell you the cost of the rebuild, because the rebuild is a different stage.

Read each line and ask what it does and what it leaves out. If the scope says nothing about baseboards, ask who removes and replaces them.

Photos and notes that go with a scope

A good scope comes with photos of the damage before work and a sketch of the rooms. The sketch shows where equipment will sit and where walls will be opened. Photos protect both sides if there is a later question about what was wet and what was removed.

Ask for a copy of the photos and the sketch. Keep them with your claim file and with the final moisture readings.

Matching the scope to the invoice

When the job ends, compare the final invoice with the scope. Each line on the invoice should map to a task or a change order. If the equipment days differ, ask for the readings that explain it. If an item appears that was not in the scope, ask when it was approved.

Do not pay a disputed line until you understand it. A clear contractor will walk you through the numbers.

Reading a scope in Derby

For Derby jobs, ask whether the scope names permits. The City of Derby lists building, electrical, mechanical, plumbing and flood plain permits, and the permits desk is at 788-6632, option 2. If the work is near a creek or in a mapped flood plain, ask the city how that affects repairs. A scope that mentions permits and who pulls them shows that the contractor is thinking about the whole project.

Payment terms and change orders

Look for the amount due at signing, at milestones and at completion. Be cautious about large up front payments. A change order is a written change to the scope. Ask that any change be approved in writing before work is done.

Signatures and authority

The drying lines in a scope should trace to ANSI/IICRC S500, the industry standard, so ask the contractor which parts of it the plan follows.

You sign to authorize work. Read the lines about insurance, assignment of benefits and direction to pay. Ask your insurer before you sign anything that gives a contractor rights to your claim. Compare the written scope with the estimate line by line.

Questions to ask before you sign

Ask what is included, what is not and what would change the price. Ask when the work will start and how long drying is expected to take. Ask who your contact is on site. Ask for a copy of the final moisture readings.

Common questions

What is a scope of work in water damage restoration?

It is the written list of tasks, materials, rooms and equipment that a contractor will provide, and the basis of the price.

What does R&R mean on a scope?

R&R means remove and replace. Make sure both the removal and the replacement are in the scope you are signing.

Should the scope list drying equipment?

Yes. It should list air movers, dehumidifiers and expected days, and how readings will be taken.

Can I change the scope after I sign?

Yes, through a written change order. Ask that any change be approved in writing before work is done.
